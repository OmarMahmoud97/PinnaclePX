# What a good website does for a small business: evidence and wording

Prepared 5 September 2026 for the home page plan, gap 2 ("nothing says what a good site does for the owner"). Every figure in this file stays in this file. The page carries the plain claims in section 4 and none of the numbers, per the owner's rule. Labels follow the repo's convention: **evidence** (a study, dataset or official document), **practitioner opinion** (a named practitioner's guide), **observed** (a live page loaded today, with the URL and what it said), **business reasoning**. Anything not confirmed from a primary source today is marked **Unverified**.

Every source below was loaded on 5 September 2026 unless the row says otherwise. Four reports arrived as PDFs that the fetch tool could not read; they were saved and extracted locally with `pdftotext`, and the page numbers given are the PDF's own page sequence from that extraction.

## 1. How to read this file

- Section 2 is the evidence, one table per theme, with the exact wording of each figure, what it measures and how far it carries for a UK small business audience.
- Section 3 turns the figures into four plain outcomes a page can claim without a number.
- Section 4 has twelve candidate sentences for the page. Four are marked **conditional**: they are true only once the owner has decided the terms of the real build (care plan, ownership, contact pattern, accessibility promise). Ship none of those four before the decision.
- Section 5 has three H2 and lead pairs, plus a spare, with a recommendation.
- Section 6 says how the section could be shown rather than told, using only components that exist, and what gates each option.
- Section 7 lists words and framings to refuse, with the rule each one breaks.
- Section 8 lists the owner decisions. Section 9 is the reading-level check. Section 10 is the fetch log.

### 2.8 The advertising rules the wording must satisfy

CAP Code (non-broadcast), section 3, read today at https://www.asa.org.uk/type/non_broadcast/code_section/03.html (**evidence**, official rules):

- 3.1 "Marketing communications must not materially mislead or be likely to do so."
- 3.3 "Marketing communications must not omit material information or information required to be included by law."
- 3.7 "Before distributing or submitting a marketing communication for publication, marketers must hold documentary evidence to prove claims that consumers are likely to regard as objective."
- 3.11 "Marketing communications must not mislead consumers by exaggerating the capability or performance of a product."
- 3.33 comparisons "must compare products meeting the same needs or intended for the same purpose"; 3.34 "They must objectively compare one or more material, relevant, verifiable and representative feature of those products."

Consequence for this section: an objective claim about outcomes ("more enquiries", "found on Google") needs documentary evidence for this studio's sites, which does not exist yet. So every sentence in section 4 is either a description of what a website does in general, backed by section 2, or a promise about what the studio will build, which the owner must be able to keep (marked **conditional**).

## 3. From figures to plain claims: the four outcomes

Business reasoning, resting on section 2. These are the four things a good website does for a small business owner that the evidence lets the page say without a number.

1. **Found.** Customers are online (95%), mostly on a phone (77% of time; one in five online adults on a phone only), and almost all use a search engine (96%). Google now indexes only what a phone can reach. A website's first job is to exist where those people look, and to work on the device they hold. What the page may not say: anything about ranking, "page one", or how many people will find them.
2. **Trusted at a glance.** People judge credibility on the look of a page first (Fogg 46.1%), within a second (Lindgaard), and decide whether to stay in the first ten seconds (Liu, White and Dumais via NN/g). Small errors and missing basics cost the visit (Harley). What the page may not say: "professional design" as a selling word, or any figure.
3. **Answers the first questions.** Up-front disclosure and comprehensive, current content are two of NN/g's four trust factors; the HomeCleanz session shows a visitor leaving in 35 seconds because a rate was not stated; the pyramid of trust says do not ask for contact until the basics are answered. This is also what the taster already promises to ask on the call: "what customers ask before they book". What the page may not say: "converts", "leads", or how many enquiries.
4. **Easy to get in touch.** Stanford guideline 5; BrightLocal's 20% who contact or book after a good review and 54% who check the website first; Google Maps on 77% of UK smartphones. What the page may not say: "24/7 lead capture" or any response-time promise the owner has not made.

Honest boundaries the section must keep: the studio has shipped no client sites yet, so no outcome sentence may be phrased as a result the studio has delivered. The sentences describe what a good website does and what the studio builds, not what it has achieved.

## 5. Three H2 and lead pairs

Every H2 ends with a full stop and is in sentence case. Each lead is under 30 words and every sentence under 20. Grades are in section 9.

**Pair A (recommended for launch)**

- H2: What a good website does for you.
- Lead (26 words): Most people find you on a phone. They decide in seconds whether you look real. Then they want answers and a way to get in touch.
- Why: it names the gap literally (the page never says what a site does for the owner) and its three sentences are the outcomes in order: found, trusted, answers and contact. "In seconds" rests on Lindgaard and NN/g without a count. Reads at about grade 3 (section 9).

**Pair B**

- H2: A website has one job.
- Lead (28 words): Turn the person who just looked you up into a booking or an enquiry. That takes a page that loads fast, looks real and answers the first questions.
- Why: the strongest business framing and the closest to the studio's own thinking ("the home page has one job"). Risk: "into a booking or an enquiry" edges towards an outcome claim; it is phrased as the job, not a result, which keeps it inside CAP 3.7. Use if the owner wants the section to argue for the real build harder.

**Pair C**

- H2: Open when you're not.
- Lead (25 words): Your site answers the questions people ask before they book. It shows you're real. It makes getting in touch one tap. All on a phone.
- Why: the tersest, and it matches the page's existing H2 rhythm ("One question at a time.", "Straight answers."). Risk: the H2 needs the lead to land, and the third sentence depends on decision D3 (one-tap contact). Hold until D3 is decided, then consider as the sequential test variant.

**Spare**

- H2: Found, trusted, contacted.
- Lead (22 words): Three things a website does for a small business. Yours should do all three on a phone, before the visitor has scrolled.
- Why: works as a label for the three-beat version of the section (6.2). Weaker as a headline because it is a list, not a sentence.

## 7. Words and framings to avoid

Each entry names the rule it breaks: **owner** (the owner rules in the brief), **CAP** (section 2.8), **NN/g** (marketese, `docs/home-page-research.md`), **terminology** (the brief's vocabulary), or **unprovable** (no documentary evidence exists for this studio).

- Any figure, percentage, "X in Y", or "most people leave after three seconds". **owner**, and the figures are global or US anyway.
- "More customers", "more enquiries", "more bookings", "grow your business", "double", "boost", "increase sales". **CAP 3.7, 3.11**, **unprovable**: the studio has no delivered sites to evidence.
- "Get found on Google", "rank", "page one", "top of Google", "SEO", "SEO-optimised", "search-engine friendly". **unprovable**, **CAP 3.11**; Google's own document says relevance comes first. Also excluded scope (SEO and discovery work).
- "Convert", "conversion", "leads", "lead capture", "funnel", "ROI", "traffic". **NN/g** marketese and jargon; the reader is a physio, not a marketer.
- "Professional" as praise ("a professional look"), "stunning", "beautiful", "modern", "sleek", "clean", "world-class", "bespoke", "premium", "cutting-edge", "seamless", "effortless", "powerful", "robust", "elevate", "unlock", "supercharge", "next-level", "stand out from the crowd". **NN/g** marketese; superlatives are banned outright by the voice rules. Note that Stanford's guideline uses "professional" descriptively; the page should show it, not say it.
- "Trust signals", "credibility", "brand", "UX", "user experience", "responsive", "mobile-optimised", "mobile-first", "CMS", "hosting", "domain", "SSL". **terminology**: say "works on a phone", "your address on the web", "we look after it", or explain in a clause.
- "Instantly", "live", "generate", "generated", "AI website builder", "AI" anywhere in this section. **owner**; "AI" is already used twice (the question and its answer) and the copy test pins that count.
- "Copy" for the visitor's words. **terminology**: "wording". The copy test fails on it.
- "24/7", "always on", "never sleeps", "works while you sleep". Cliché, and "never" is an absolute the voice rules refuse. "Open when you're not" is the plain form and is defensible.
- "Ten seconds", "three seconds", "50 milliseconds", "first impression in under a second". Time claims are figures in disguise. **owner**. "In seconds" is the limit.
- "Your competitors have one", "everyone else has a website", "you're losing customers to". **unprovable** about any specific reader; **CAP 3.1**. UKBDS supports "most small businesses already have a website", nothing about the reader's rivals.
- "Customers trust a website more than social media", "people won't trust a business without a website", "76% visit the site before the shop". **Unverified** marketing-blog figures (2.6); do not use even without the number.
- Any sentence naming Wix, Squarespace, Durable or Mixo as worse, "cookie-cutter", "template sites", "cheap builders". **CAP 3.33 to 3.34**: a comparison must be objective and verifiable. That belongs to gap 5 and must be written as a fair, checkable difference (who designs the layout, who writes the wording), never as denigration.
- "Guaranteed", "risk-free", "no-risk", "proven". **CAP 3.7**, **unprovable**. "Free" and "nothing to cancel" are the true, existing claims and are enough.
- "Our clients", "businesses like yours have seen", "case study", any testimonial or logo. **owner**: there are no clients yet, and the example businesses are labelled "Example brief, not a client".
- Exclamation marks, em dashes, Title Case in headings, American spellings ("color", "optimize"). **voice rules**; the copy test catches the em dash and the reading-level script the sentence length.
