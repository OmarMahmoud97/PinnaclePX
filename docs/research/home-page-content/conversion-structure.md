# Conversion structure research: the eight new sections

Prepared 5 September 2026 for the PinnaclePX home page plan. Question: for a service business landing page (a studio selling a built website, not SaaS), what do the evidence and the best practitioner guidance say about (a) an outcomes section without numbers or testimonials, (b) a who-it-is-for section, (c) a what-is-included and how-long process section, (d) an honest comparison against DIY builders and agencies, (e) an after-launch care section, (f) a low-commitment path that is not an email capture, (g) risk reversal for a paid build, and (h) where each sits in the persuasion arc.

Conventions (the repo's own): **evidence** = a study, dataset or official document; **practitioner** = a named practitioner's guide; **observed** = a live page loaded today with the URL; **business reasoning** = ours. Anything not read at source today is marked **Unverified**. Every URL below was fetched or searched on 5 September 2026 unless stated. Where a fetch failed, it says so.

This builds on `docs/home-page-research.md` and `docs/home-page-design-research.md` (2 and 3 September 2026) and does not repeat what they established: the fold and scanning figures, the Unbounce reading-level figures, the Stanford credibility guidelines, the Copyhackers click trigger, the Navattic gating figures, and the competitor sweep of Durable, Mixo, Relume and 10Web. Those are cited by reference where they bear on a new section.

## 0. The answer in one paragraph

The sources agree on the shape of the argument and disagree on almost nothing. Order is value, then proof, then process, then objections, then the ask (Julian Shapiro, Harry Dry, Copyhackers, April Dunford; the NN/g trust hierarchy gives the same order from the user's side). Every new section must earn its place against Harry Dry's test ("would this help me sell in person?") and Unbounce's finding that word count and reading time correlate negatively with conversion, so the eight gaps should become about three or four sections, not eight. The evidence is strongest for (f): do not ask for personal information before the visitor has seen something (NN/g trust hierarchy, Baymard account-creation study, Navattic ungated demos), and give the not-ready visitor a secondary action that is a micro conversion, not a gate. The evidence is weakest for (e) and (g): nothing rigorous exists on care-plan sections, and the guarantee literature is retail, not services; UK law (Consumer Rights Act 2015) and the CAP Code set the honest boundaries instead. For (d), the CAP Code and NN/g together say: compare approaches on verifiable attributes the visitor cares about, show only the differences, name nobody you cannot substantiate a claim about, and never denigrate. For (a) and (b), the practitioners say specifics and self-recognition ("that's so me"); the evidence says objective language and no forced self-identification.

## 1. What each source actually says

Grouped by source so the plan can cite them. Quotes are verbatim from today's fetch unless marked.

### 1.1 Evidence

**NN/g, Comparison Tables (Kate Moran and Taylor Dykes, 9 February 2024).** Comparison tables suit "compensatory decision making" over small sets of alternatives; "Use Comparison Tables for Up to 5 Items"; static tables at most 5 options, mobile at most 2. Avoid them when items are not mutually exclusive, "simple or aesthetically-driven", "inexpensive or easily replaceable", or "unique or difficult to compare". "The biggest problem with most comparison tables isn't design, it's content." "Above all else, do the work for the consumer." Layout: products as columns, attributes as rows, short text not sentences, clear row separation. https://www.nngroup.com/articles/comparison-tables/

**NN/g, Explicitly State the Difference Between Options (Taylor Dykes, 23 August 2024).** "When the key differences between choices are implied or buried, users often select the wrong option or misunderstand the features." "Without explicit differences, users are likely to assume all choices are the same or fret over the smallest distinctions." Highlight the few attributes that differ; when many differ, spotlight the important ones and use progressive disclosure for the rest. "You are likely not the person making these important decisions on your website", so do not assume the visitor knows your terms. https://www.nngroup.com/articles/explicit-differences/

**Baymard, 4 Ways to Optimize the Comparison Feature for Scanning (19 October 2022).** Remove identical attributes (an "Only show differences" toggle helped); group attributes by category rather than alphabetically ("I'm just better able to organize in my head what features I'm actually looking at"); persist column headings while scrolling; use horizontal styling so users do not "lose their place". Ecommerce context. https://baymard.com/blog/user-friendly-comparison-tools

**NN/g, Audience-Based Navigation: 5 Reasons to Avoid It (Katie Sherwin, 7 September 2015).** "Users don't know which group to choose"; labels are ambiguous (about a group or for a group); "Forcing people to self-identify creates an additional step and takes people out of their task mindset"; people worry they are missing better options offered to other segments; content duplicates. It works only when content is genuinely unique to a group. To signal audience without segmenting: keep topic-based primary navigation, use clarifying language ("For Medical Professionals" beats "Medical Professionals"). https://www.nngroup.com/articles/audience-based-navigation/

**NN/g, Hierarchy of Trust: The 5 Experiential Levels of Website Commitment (Katie Sherwin, 6 March 2016).** Levels: 1 baseline relevance and trust; 2 interest and preference over alternatives; 3 trust with personal information; 4 trust with sensitive or financial information; 5 an ongoing relationship. "Don't make demands at higher levels of commitment until you've addressed all the trust needs at the inferior levels." "The more information or effort a site asks for, the more trust and comfort the user must have." "Without building an appropriate foundation, level by level, further efforts to persuade or convert are on shaky ground." Cautionary examples: Thredup asking for email or social login from users at level 0; Nextdoor asking for a street address at level 1. https://www.nngroup.com/articles/commitment-levels/

**NN/g, Macro vs. Micro Conversions (Tim Neusesser, 19 July 2024).** A macro conversion is "a desired user action that directly contributes to the primary goals of your business". Micro conversions are of two kinds: "process-milestone" ("linear movement toward a macro conversion") and "secondary-action" ("micro conversions that do not directly lead up to a macro conversion but may predict future macro conversions"). Design and measure for secondary actions because the users who take them are more likely to convert later. The article cites an average macro conversion rate of 2.9% across industries (its figure, source not checked). https://www.nngroup.com/articles/micro-conversions/

**NN/g, The Principle of Commitment and Behavioral Consistency (Therese Fessenden, 2018).** Cites Cialdini: "people will go out of their way to behave consistently". Small first acts lead to larger ones: Yelp lets people start a review before creating an account; 23andMe's short health survey leads to longer questionnaires. Caveat: understand commitment levels and "minimize cognitive load before requesting personal information". https://www.nngroup.com/articles/commitment-consistency-ux/

**NN/g, Needy Design Patterns (Kate Moran and Kim Flaherty, 15 May 2016).** Exit-intent popups ("Before you go…!") and attention-seeking tab titles interfere with "page parking" (opening several pages for one task) and "parallel browsing". "Nobody likes a needy website"; short-term gains damage long-term trust. Relevant here because a comparison shopper opens Wix, an agency and this page in tabs and comes back later. https://www.nngroup.com/articles/needy-design-patterns/

**NN/g, "About Us" Information on Websites (Anna Kaley and Jakob Nielsen, 26 May 2019).** Users expect "a heightened level of authenticity and transparency", "plain, easy-to-understand language" and "a clear explanation of what your company does". Credibility is eroded by "stock photography", "walls of text", "hard-to-find critical information (such as what the company does and whom to contact)" and "aggressive sales tactics (e.g., promotions, banner ads, pushy tones)". Users verify claims through external reviews rather than trusting the site alone. https://www.nngroup.com/articles/about-us-information-on-websites/

**NN/g, Trustworthiness in Web Design: 4 Credibility Factors (Aurora Harley, 8 May 2016).** Design quality; "upfront disclosure" of pricing, fees, policies and contact details (a participant rejected a cleaning service in 35 seconds: "I would definitely not use HomeCleanz because they don't state the rate here"); "comprehensive, correct, and current content" that shows the full range of what you do, not only polished end results; "connected to the rest of the web" (third-party reviews trusted more than a company's own testimonials). https://www.nngroup.com/articles/trustworthy-design/

**NN/g, Show Prices for Common Scenarios (Jakob Nielsen, 9 April 2006).** When exact prices cannot be shown, show "sample prices for a set of typical orders" or "representative cases and their prices"; a configurator that needs many inputs is "much too complex and error prone" for "a quick, general idea of a service's cost". Price is off this page by owner rule, so the transferable lesson is the method: a representative scenario beats a calculator or a vague "it depends". https://www.nngroup.com/articles/show-prices-for-common-scenarios/

**NN/g, FAQs Still Deliver Great Value (Susan Farrell, 21 December 2014).** "FAQs, when done well, deliver a lot of value not only to your website visitor but also to your organization." They support purchase decisions on "credibility and product maturity". Warns against fabricated questions, a long-listed web design mistake: "If you're listening to your users, you know what their questions are." "People don't search for your solution, they search for their problem." Link the FAQ from the footer. https://www.nngroup.com/articles/faqs-deliver-value/

**NN/g, State of UX 2026 (Kate Moran, Raluca Budiu, Sarah Gibbons, 16 January 2026).** "Lazy AI features and AI slop are now ubiquitous, and the shine is fading fast." "People who've been burned by AI features are more hesitant to adopt new ones." Trust needs "transparency, control, consistency, and support when the system fails". Winners "treat AI as a tool that recedes into the background"; "authentic, human details will set experiences apart." https://www.nngroup.com/articles/state-of-ux-2026/

**Wang, Sturgis and de Kadt, "AI labeling reduces the perceived accuracy of online content but has limited broader effects" (arXiv 2506.16202, June 2025, revised February 2026).** A nationally representative sample of 3,861. Labelling news articles as AI-produced reduced perceived accuracy; the effect was mitigated when people were made more aware of how AI was used; the authors conclude "transparency policies may benefit from contextualizing AI use to mitigate unintended public skepticism." Context is news content, not web design, so the transfer to "say AI once, and say exactly what it does" is business reasoning. https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.16202

**Buell and Norton, "The Labor Illusion: How Operational Transparency Increases Perceived Value", Management Science 57(9), 2011.** The publisher page and the HBS page both returned 403 today, and the Semantic Scholar record has the abstract elided, so the following is from search-index snippets and is **Unverified as to wording**: when a site signals that it is exerting effort, people can prefer a longer wait to instant results even when the results are identical; five experiments in online travel and online dating; perceived effort induces reciprocity, which raises valuation. Supports the existing "While you answer, we're already working" line and any visible build process. https://pubsonline.informs.org/doi/10.1287/mnsc.1110.1376

**Moorthy and Srinivasan, "Signaling Quality with a Money-Back Guarantee: The Role of Transaction Costs", Marketing Science 14(4), 1995, pp. 442 to 466.** Publisher page 403; abstract elided in Semantic Scholar. From snippets, **Unverified as to wording**: a money-back guarantee can signal quality because lower-quality products are returned more often and cost the seller more; the signal works when the consumer's transaction costs are low. Retail goods, not services. https://pubsonline.informs.org/doi/10.1287/mksc.14.4.442

**ESMT Berlin, "The economics of the money-back guarantee" (Tamer Boyaci and Yalcin Akcay, 1 August 2017).** Money-back guarantees raise sales by removing perceived risk and can be profitable when returned items are resold as open-box stock. Retail; the resale mechanism has no analogue for a built website. Cited in the design research already; read at source today. https://esmt.berlin/knowledge/research-insights/economics-money-back-guarantee

**Navattic, State of the Interactive Product Demo 2026.** Top 1% of 40,000+ demos, each with at least 100 engaged users a year. "Ungated shows 6% higher engagement, 7% higher completion": ungated 58% engagement and 49% completion against gated 52% and 42%. Of gated demos, "75% use just one field"; 45% place the gate last. "80% of top performing demo CTAs are visible above the fold or in the navbar." The 66% ungated share comes from a search snippet of the same report and matches the repo's earlier read. Software demos, not services. https://www.navattic.com/report/state-of-the-interactive-product-demo-2026

**Baymard, Save Account Creation for the Confirmation Step (42% Don't) (19 September 2023).** "42% of sites" ask users to create an account at the start of checkout; participants "hesitated and stopped their checkout progression", perceived it as a burden, and some mistook an optional prompt for a mandatory one. Recommendation: ask after the order is placed and give three to five benefits then. Ecommerce, but the mechanism (personal information demanded before value is delivered) is the same as an email gate. https://baymard.com/blog/delayed-account-creation

**Baymard, Have Direct Links to Returns and Shipping Info (16 January 2019).** A subset of users habitually go to the footer for policy information ("I go to the bottom because that's where it usually has all those things"); 20% of sites lack direct footer links; without them users go "information hunting" and some skip the same information on the product page. Ecommerce; the transfer to ownership, hosting and privacy answers in the footer is business reasoning. https://baymard.com/blog/footer-needs-return-shipping-links

**Unbounce, Conversion Benchmark Report (2024 edition, still current on the site today).** 41,000+ pages, 464M visits, 57M conversions. Median 6.6%. Grade 5 to 7 copy converts at 11.1%, professional-level at 5.3%; difficult words −24.3%, reading time −19.4%, word count −18.6% correlations with conversion; 83% of visits on mobile; desktop converts 8% better. The report "does not provide specific data on form length or number of CTAs". **Unverified and not to be used:** third-party statistics roundups found today attribute a "2026 Conversion Benchmark Report" to Unbounce with figures such as a median of 8.1%, single-CTA pages at 13.5% and form-field curves; a search restricted to unbounce.com finds no such edition. https://unbounce.com/conversion-benchmark-report/

**UK Business Data Survey 2026 (DSIT, published 18 June 2026).** Businesses with a website: sole traders 76%, micro 84%, small 92%, medium 97%, large 99%. The report gives no breakdown of what businesses use a website for, so no evidence about "enquiries" or "found on Google" comes from this source. https://www.gov.uk/government/statistics/uk-business-data-survey-2026/uk-business-data-survey-2026

**CAP Code, Section 3: Misleading advertising (ASA, non-broadcast).** 3.1 "Marketing communications must not materially mislead or be likely to do so." 3.2 "Obvious exaggerations ('puffery') and claims that the average consumer who sees the marketing communication is unlikely to take literally are allowed provided they do not materially mislead." 3.3 "Marketing communications must not omit material information." 3.7 marketers "must hold documentary evidence to prove claims that consumers are likely to regard as objective". 3.11 "must not mislead consumers by exaggerating the capability or performance of a product." 3.12 "must not present rights given to consumers in law as a distinctive feature of the marketer's offer." Comparisons with identifiable competitors: 3.33 "must compare products meeting the same needs or intended for the same purpose"; 3.34 "must objectively compare one or more material, relevant, verifiable and representative feature"; 3.35 no confusion between marketer and competitor. 3.37 comparisons with an unidentifiable competitor "must not mislead" and "must not be selected to give the marketer an unrepresentative advantage". 3.41 "must not discredit or denigrate another product, marketer, trade mark". Guarantees: 3.55 "must not use the word 'guarantee' in a way that could cause confusion about a consumer's rights"; 3.56 "must not mislead by omitting significant limitations to an advertised guarantee"; 3.57 refund valid money-back claims promptly. https://www.asa.org.uk/type/non_broadcast/code_section/03.html

**Consumer Rights Act 2015, Part 1 Chapter 4 (services).** s49 "the trader must perform the service with reasonable care and skill." s50 "anything that is said or written to the consumer, by or on behalf of the trader, about the trader or the service" is a term of the contract if the consumer took it into account. s51 where no price is agreed, "a reasonable price for the service, and no more." s52 "within a reasonable time." s54 to s56 remedies: repeat performance and price reduction (including a refund of anything paid above the reduced amount). s57 liability for these cannot be excluded. Two consequences for the page: every scope or timeline line on the home page becomes a contract term for a consumer client (s50), and any "guarantee" must add to these rights to be worth stating (CAP 3.12). https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/2015/15/part/1/chapter/4

## 2. Findings by section

Each subsection gives what the evidence says, what practitioners say, what not to do, and the implication for this page. Implications are business reasoning unless labelled.

### (a) Outcomes, or "why this matters", without numbers or testimonials

- **Evidence.** Objective language raised measured usability by 27% and users "detested marketese" (NN/g, cited in the repo's research of 2 September). Grade 5 to 7 copy converts at twice the rate of professional copy; word count correlates negatively (Unbounce 2024). CAP 3.7 requires documentary evidence for claims consumers would take as objective, and 3.11 bans exaggerating performance, so an outcomes section without numbers is also the compliant one. CAP 3.2 permits "puffery" only where nobody would take it literally, which is not how a wary buyer reads a claim like "more customers".
- **Practitioner.** Sell the outcome, not the artefact: "People don't get excited by a new headlamp. They get excited by new adventures." "You can't bullshit specifics" (Harry Dry). Every feature ties back to one dominant value proposition (Julian Shapiro). Value is the "so what for customers" of the unique attributes (Dunford). Comprehensive content that shows the range of what you do, not only polished end results, signals expertise (NN/g trustworthiness).
- **Do not.** Superlatives; invented statistics or "typical results"; a result stated as a promise ("you will get more enquiries"), which CAP 3.11 and CRA s50 both turn against you; adjectives where a concrete scene would do.
- **Implication.** Write outcomes as what the site lets the owner's customers do, in scenes the owner recognises: find the business on a phone, see what it does and where, see prices or the way to ask, book or ring without a form fight, trust it enough to turn up. Each scene should map to something the three designs already show (services, area, a booking action), so the outcome is demonstrated by the taster rather than claimed. Frame the "why it matters" from the owner's week (the questions they answer by phone that the site should answer for them) rather than from marketing outcomes. Four short items is the practitioner range (Julian: 3 to 6). Keep every sentence falsifiable and hedged only by grammar ("so customers can..."), not by weasel words.

### (b) Who it is for

- **Evidence.** Forcing visitors to pick their segment costs a step, breaks task focus and makes them worry that other segments get a better deal (NN/g audience-based navigation). Clarifying language ("For X") works where segmented navigation does not. 76% of UK sole traders and 84% of micro businesses already have a website (UKBDS 2026), so "I already have a site and it embarrasses me" is the majority situation, not "I have no site".
- **Practitioner.** Best-fit customers are the ones who care most about the differentiated value, here seeing designs before committing (Dunford). Self-recognition comes from specific scenarios ("that's so me": Copyhackers), from a qualifier that names the reader up front (Copyhackers 15-pt, Section 1), and from a "Perfect for you if" close (Section 14). Julian's persona routing is for products with several major personas and is a top-of-page choice, which NN/g's evidence argues against for this audience. "Own your niche" and "write the title only you can" (Harry Dry).
- **Do not.** Audience tabs or a segment picker; a list of trades that excludes anyone not on it; example businesses that read as clients; a single persona so narrow that the partner reading the forwarded link feels excluded.
- **Implication.** Segment by situation, not by trade: burned by an agency before; quoted more than the business can justify; started on a builder and stalled; a first site for a new business. Three or four items in the owner's own words, with the approved example briefs (VetPres, Mvmnt, Go Wild Dog Walking, each labelled "Example brief, not a client") as the concrete trades. An honest "probably not for you if" line (a large shop with hundreds of products, a web app) is the Dunford "pros and cons of alternatives" move and raises credibility with the sceptic; it is an owner decision. One naming line under the H1 is the low-cost version (the design research already proposed it); the full block belongs after the examples so the reader meets the segments with a design in view.

### (c) What is included, how long it takes, what happens after you say yes

- **Evidence.** Upfront disclosure of cost, policies and process is one of four credibility factors; a participant rejected a service within 35 seconds for not stating its rate (NN/g trustworthiness). With price off the page by owner rule, everything else that is disclosable must be, or the section repeats the hidden-rate failure in another form. Where an exact figure cannot be given, a representative scenario beats a configurator or "it depends" (NN/g, show prices for common scenarios; the transfer from price to timeline is reasoning). Showing the work raises perceived value and trust (Buell and Norton, wording unverified). Under CRA 2015 s50 anything written about the service that the client relied on becomes a contract term, and s52 requires a reasonable time; CAP 3.3 forbids omitting material information.
- **Practitioner.** "In a perfect world, we'd want a solution that was..." aligns the buyer on the requirements before the offer (Dunford). Raise ongoing needs early, not at the end (Van Deusen). "Would this help me sell in person?" (Harry Dry). Dunford's finding that buyers fear messing up more than missing out is cited in the design research from Lenny's Newsletter; not re-read today.
- **Do not.** "Bespoke", "tailored" and other words that describe nothing; a timeline the studio cannot keep in a holiday week (it becomes a contract term); a scope list padded with things every site has; a process that stops at launch.
- **Implication.** One numbered process from "you like a design" to "your site is live", each step saying who does what and how long it typically takes, as a range from `lib/config.ts`: the call and fixed quote; what the owner supplies (photos, prices, a logo file); the build from the chosen design; a review round with a stated number of changes; launch on the owner's domain; then what happens after (section e). State who writes the words (a person, from the call), that it works on a phone, and who owns the site, domain and words at the end. Give a representative scenario ("a five-page site for a clinic usually takes about N weeks") rather than a matrix. This section is where CRA s50 bites hardest, so every line needs the owner's confirmation before it ships.

### (d) An honest comparison against DIY builders and agencies

- **Evidence.** Comparison tables help compensatory decisions over up to five options, and the failure is usually content, not layout (NN/g comparison tables); when differences are buried, "users often select the wrong option" or "assume all choices are the same" (NN/g explicit differences); show only the differences and group attributes (Baymard). NN/g cautions against tables for items that are "unique or difficult to compare": a builder, an agency and this studio differ in kind, so the table must compare on attributes the buyer actually decides on, not on features. Under the CAP Code, a comparison with an identifiable competitor must be objective, on "material, relevant, verifiable and representative" features (3.34), must not confuse (3.35) or denigrate (3.41), and needs documentary evidence (3.7); a comparison with unidentifiable competitors must not select features "to give the marketer an unrepresentative advantage" (3.37). Statutory rights must not be presented as a distinctive feature (3.12). At level 2 of the trust hierarchy the site must show why it is preferable to the alternatives before it may ask for anything (NN/g).
- **Practitioner.** Positioning starts from competitive alternatives, including doing nothing (Dunford). Discuss the pros and cons of each approach without naming a competitor ("I didn't even say the name") and get agreement on the perfect solution before pitching (Dunford). Handle "there are cheaper alternatives" by explaining the price and comparing (CXL). Study competitors' sites to differentiate (Julian).
- **Do not.** Name Wix or Squarespace next to claims you cannot document; a tick-and-cross grid that gives the studio every tick (3.37); "templates everyone uses" (unverifiable, and nine of this studio's ten templates are placeholders today); anything that reads as denigration; more than five columns; a table on a phone with more than two columns visible.
- **Implication.** A three-column "your options" section by approach, not by brand: do it yourself with a builder; hire an agency; this studio. Five or six rows, each a question the visitor asks: who designs it; who writes the words; when you first see something; what you pay before you see anything; who does the work; who owns it afterwards; what happens after launch. Every cell for the other two approaches must be a fair, general description a Wix or agency owner would accept ("you build it from a template, in your own time"; "you see a first draft after you have paid a deposit"). Give the other approaches genuine pros (cheapest; full team for a big project) so the sceptic trusts the column about the studio. If a brand is named, name it only in a neutral definition ("a builder such as Wix or Squarespace"). On phones, render the same content as three stacked cards, one per approach. The planned straight answer "Will it look like everyone else's?" stays gated on all ten templates, as the design plan already records.

### (e) After launch: hosting, updates, support

- **Evidence.** None specific. The applicable evidence is general: users expect disclosure of ongoing fees and policies (NN/g trustworthiness), look for policy information in the footer and go hunting when it is missing (Baymard footer links), and rate "hard-to-find critical information" as a credibility failure (NN/g About Us). CAP 3.3 (material information), 3.55 and 3.56 (guarantee wording and limits) govern the copy. CRA s57 means liability for reasonable care cannot be excluded whatever the plan says.
- **Practitioner.** Raise maintenance early, explain it plainly, offer it as a choice, never as a threat; keep SEO and other disciplines out of it (Van Deusen).
- **Do not.** "Your site will break without us"; "hosting included" without saying for how long or what happens after; "unlimited" anything; a care plan that the visitor cannot leave; any recurring cost hidden until the call while the page says "fixed quote".
- **Implication.** This is a buyer question and the studio's recurring revenue, so it belongs in two places: one step in the process section ("after launch: we host it, keep it updated and fix anything that breaks; you can take it elsewhere at any time") and one FAQ item that answers the four questions directly: who hosts it, who owns the domain, what an update means, and what happens if the owner leaves. No price (owner rule), but the existence of a monthly option must be disclosed so the fixed quote is not later read as misleading. Add "Hosting and updates" to the footer's studio group, since that is where Baymard's users look.

### (f) A low-commitment path for the not-ready visitor, without an email capture

- **Evidence.** This is the best-evidenced section. Do not demand level-3 commitment (personal information) from a visitor at level 0 or 1 (NN/g hierarchy of trust). Asking for account creation before value is delivered made participants hesitate and stop, and some read an optional prompt as mandatory (Baymard, 42% of sites). Ungated demos beat gated ones on engagement (58% vs 52%) and completion (49% vs 42%); where gates exist, 75% take one field and 45% gate last (Navattic 2026). Secondary-action micro conversions predict later macro conversions and should be designed for and measured (NN/g). Small first acts (starting a Yelp review, a short 23andMe survey) lead to larger ones (NN/g, Cialdini). Visitors park pages in tabs and return; exit popups and tab tricks damage trust (NN/g needy patterns). "People are more likely to take action when the effort is small" (Growth.Design).
- **Practitioner.** A second CTA with the luxury of space to "remind the customer why they're clicking" (Harry Dry). Ask for information incrementally (Unbounce, the breadcrumb technique). "No one likes a pushy chatbot" (Unbounce).
- **Do not.** A newsletter or "get the guide" gate; an exit-intent popup; a chat widget; a "save for later" that needs an email; any path that quietly becomes a lead form; a second primary button on the same screen (Growth.Design's too-many-CTAs).
- **Implication.** The not-ready visitor needs three things, all already true of the product or cheap to add. First, something to look at: the examples gallery, opening a real rendered preview from a labelled example brief, is the "see something" path, and it costs the visitor nothing. Gate it on templates as the design plan says; with one template built, the honest version is one example brief with one design (`/examples/aurora`) labelled as such, and the section grows to three and nine as templates land. Second, a way to come back: the URL. Say so plainly ("Come back when you're ready. Nothing here expires.") rather than building a save mechanism. Third, a way to bring the partner in: a "Send this page to whoever decides with you" action using the Web Share API where available and a prefilled mailto or copied link elsewhere, with no capture and no account. Tag shared links with `utm_source=share` as the design plan already proposes for preview links. Place the path after the examples ("Not ready? Open an example, or send this page to a partner") and again in the closing section, never in the hero. Measure `example_open {business}` and a new `share_click {location}` as secondary-action micro conversions, and read them as leading indicators, not as failures to start.

### (g) Risk reversal and guarantees for the paid build

- **Evidence.** Guarantees move sales because "usually the vendor has the buyer carry most of the risk" (CXL, practitioner) and a money-back guarantee can signal quality when the buyer's cost of using it is low (Moorthy and Srinivasan, wording unverified); the rigorous studies are about returnable goods and do not transfer cleanly to a built website that cannot be returned. UK law already gives a consumer client reasonable care and skill, a reasonable time, binding written statements, repeat performance and price reduction (CRA 2015 s49 to s56), so a "satisfaction guarantee" that restates these adds nothing and CAP 3.12 forbids presenting it as distinctive. CAP 3.55 and 3.56 require that "guarantee" not confuse rights and that limitations be stated.
- **Practitioner.** A guarantee should be branded, give its length, say what the buyer will do in that time, what they should see, and "the EXACT process for getting a refund" (Copyhackers 15-pt). The strongest form compensates the pain, not just the price (CXL, "The Punctual Plumber"). Positioning against "do nothing" matters because 40% of deals go to no decision (Dunford, second-hand).
- **Do not.** Use the word "guarantee" for anything a customer already has by law; a money-back promise the studio cannot afford after weeks of work; "100% satisfaction"; a guarantee with limits that appear only on the call; any risk reversal the owner has not agreed in writing.
- **Implication.** The free, ungated three designs are already the strongest risk reversal this business can offer: try before you talk, and see before you pay. The page should name that plainly as the answer to "What's the catch?" (it already does). For the paid build, the honest options that fit a one-person studio, in rough order of strength for this audience: nothing is paid until the homepage design is agreed; a fixed quote in writing that cannot change unless the scope does; a written timeline with a stated remedy if the studio misses it (the Punctual Plumber form, only if the owner will honour it); everything (site, domain, words) is the client's at the end whatever happens. Say it once in the taster's third step and once in the FAQ price item, next to the call button, because that is where the buyer weighs the risk. Whatever is chosen must be true before it ships, must state its limits, and should avoid the word "guarantee" unless it goes beyond the Act. Owner decision required.

### (h) Where each belongs in the persuasion arc

The practitioner orders agree: Julian Shapiro (hero, social proof, CTA, features and objections, repeat CTA); Harry Dry (features and objections, proof, FAQ, second CTA, founder's note); Copyhackers (qualifier first, trust and pain before product, guarantee after product and before FAQ, "perfect for you if" as a second close, risk mitigator last); Dunford (insight, alternatives, perfect world, differentiated value, proof, objections, ask). The NN/g trust hierarchy is the same order seen from the visitor: relevance and trust, then preference over alternatives, then personal information. Two constraints from the evidence: every added section costs reading time and word count, which correlate negatively with conversion (Unbounce), and each must pass "would this help me sell in person?" (Harry Dry). So the eight gaps should land as three or four sections plus additions to existing ones, not eight new bands.

## 3. What the sources say not to do, consolidated

- Ask for an email, an account or any personal information before the visitor has seen something (NN/g trust hierarchy; Baymard; Navattic). The current design already obeys this; the new sections must not add a gate.
- Exit popups, attention-seeking tab titles, pushy chat (NN/g needy patterns; Unbounce).
- Forced segment selection or audience navigation (NN/g).
- A comparison that names competitors with unverifiable claims, cherry-picks attributes, or denigrates (CAP 3.7, 3.34, 3.37, 3.41). More than five columns or more than two on a phone (NN/g).
- Presenting statutory rights as your guarantee; the word "guarantee" without its limits (CAP 3.12, 3.55, 3.56).
- Fabricated FAQ questions (NN/g). Marketese, superlatives and abstract imagery (NN/g; Julian; Harry Dry).
- Timelines or scope lines that are not always true: they become contract terms (CRA s50) and misleading omissions (CAP 3.3).
- Several competing calls to action on one screen (Growth.Design); every extra block that would not help in a face-to-face sale (Harry Dry); more words than the argument needs (Unbounce).
- Care plans sold as a threat (Van Deusen).
- Claiming results ("more enquiries") as outcomes (CAP 3.11); invented numbers (owner rule and CAP 3.7).
- Third-party "2026 Unbounce" statistics: not on Unbounce's site; do not cite.

## 4. Gaps in the evidence, stated plainly

- No controlled study was found on outcomes sections, who-it-is-for sections, comparison sections or care-plan sections for service businesses. The guidance for (a), (b), (d) and (e) is inferred from general usability evidence, practitioner consensus and UK advertising law.
- The guarantee literature is retail and theoretical. Whether any risk reversal changes booked calls for a web studio cannot be known from sources; it can only be measured after a baseline, one change at a time, as the existing plan already prescribes.
- No UK source was found that says what small business owners want a website to do (enquiries, being found, bookings). The UKBDS 2026 gives ownership rates only. Any such line on the page is the owner's own knowledge, not evidence.
- The Navattic and Baymard figures come from software demos and ecommerce checkouts. The mechanism transfers; the numbers do not.
- Two academic abstracts and every CXL blog page were unreachable today; those citations rest on snippets and the CXL PDF and are marked.
