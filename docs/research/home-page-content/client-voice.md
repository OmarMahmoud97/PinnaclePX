# Client voice: what UK small business owners say when they buy a website

Researched 5 September 2026 for the PinnaclePX home page plan. Written from the buyer's side.

## How to read this

- Every quote below is as returned by the fetch tool when asked for verbatim text from a live page loaded today. Treat them as verbatim or near-verbatim; spelling and grammar are the poster's own. Each carries the page URL, the date of the post or review, and a label.
- Labels: **evidence** (a study, dataset or official document), **practitioner opinion** (a named practitioner's guide), **observed** (a live page loaded today, with URL and what it showed), **business reasoning** (mine). **Unverified** marks anything I could not confirm on the source page.
- Many forum threads are old (2010 to 2018). The fears have not changed; the platforms named have. Where a thread is old I say so. The Trustpilot reviews are 2021 to 2026, and several are from the last month.
- What I could not reach: reddit.com blocks this crawler entirely (every reddit.com search and fetch was refused), so the Reddit threads the brief asked for are absent and UK Business Forums and MoneySavingExpert stand in for them. Google reviews are not fetchable. The FSB press release page returned no content, so its figures come from press coverage. The Shopify Community thread on AI copy returned 404 twice and appears only as an **Unverified** search snippet. No Which? survey on this subject exists as far as I could find.

## The one thing to take away

The single sentence closest to PinnaclePX's whole proposition was written by a small business owner looking for a new designer after a bad first one (UK Business Forums, 18 November 2011, observed): they wanted "ideas and maybe some screenshots from the new designer about how they may go forward before I start paying them", and the reassurance that they would "get the right help as I dont want to have to do it again". Every guide in this file tells buyers to demand live sites, not screenshots; to open them on a phone; to read the words. The taster is the studio's portfolio, and the page should treat it as one.

## Part 1. The themes, in the buyer's words

Each theme has: the phrases harvested, the one sentence a burned buyer needs to read (plain British English, under 20 words, written to the owner's rules), what they would want to SEE to believe it, and what the current page already does about it.

What sits under the fear: not the number itself but not knowing what the number buys, surprise charges after the quote (VAT, hosting, renewals, "small changes"), and paying before seeing anything.

- A list of what the full site includes, so the quote has a shape even without a number: pages, wording, photos, mobile, hosting for a stated period, domain in their name. Scope is allowed on the page; price is not.
- A plain statement that nothing is paid before the designs, before the call, or before the quote is agreed.
- Renewal honesty: what recurs after launch and what does not (see theme 9).

Already on the page: FAQ "What does the full site cost?" (fixed quote on the call); the call agenda. Missing: scope, and "what recurs".

What sits under the fear: paying for design and getting a template someone else also has; and a site that says "elevate your business" like every other.

The sentence, gated to what exists today: **Every layout is built by hand. Your logo, your colours and your own words make it yours.**

When ten templates render: **Ten layouts, each built by hand. Your logo, your colours and your words on every one.**

What they would want to SEE

- Two different layouts of the same example brief, side by side, so difference between layouts is visible rather than claimed. Not possible honestly until template two ships; the plan should gate it.

- Wording that is specific to the example business, not filler. The VetPres brief's own sentence on the built page does this.
- Later, a straight answer "Will it look like everyone else's?" once the honest answer is "no, and here are the ten".

Already on the page: "Three designs, not one. Different layouts, so you can see what suits you." Missing: any finished design, so the claim has no picture behind it.

## Part 2. How buyers judge a portfolio and examples

What the guides tell buyers to do, and what buyers say they do

1. Demand live sites, not pictures. "show real, live websites they've built — not mock-ups, not Figma screens" (36 Visuals, https://36visuals.co.uk/web-agency-red-flags/); "Request actual functioning sites built for UK clients, not mockups or screenshots" (Ron Studios); "If they can't show you live websites they've built — not mockups, not screenshots, but actual functioning sites — that's a problem" (Launchwork). All practitioner opinion.
2. Open them on a phone first. "Open the site on your phone." (Baslon Digital); "Open live sites on mobile and check: load speed (under 3 seconds on 4G)" (WebKatie). Practitioner opinion.
3. Read the words. "Read the pages. Look for typos, inconsistent spacing, stretched images, odd hover states, or clumsy button labels." and "Can a first-time visitor understand what the business does and what to do next? Clear hierarchy beats visual flair." (Baslon Digital). Practitioner opinion.
4. Look for range. "Variety of styles (not just one look repeated)" and "real business websites rather than concept designs" (WebKatie); "A portfolio of work that all looks the same" is the warning (Northern Sauce). Practitioner opinion.
5. Ask for work like mine. Request "3 recent sites for businesses like mine" with what was built and why (Northern Sauce). Practitioner opinion.
6. Check the sites are still alive. Whether sites "remain active and ranked" (WebKatie). Practitioner opinion.
7. Ask for the whole portfolio. "Ask for a full portfolio and don't trust if they say they have just a few because the rest can't be seen" (UK Business Forums buyer thread, via search summary, **Unverified** wording). "The main thing that will differentiate web guys is their portfolio." UK Business Forums, 28 Nov 2012, https://www.ukbusinessforums.co.uk/threads/how-to-find-a-website-designer-developer.279366/ (observed).
8. Talk to past clients. "Take a look at their portfolio like you say and also speak to past clients" (UK Business Forums, 2011); "Ask any potential web designers for details for some of their clients, and then speak to them" (UK Business Forums, 2012). Observed.
9. Check who does the work. "a tier 1 dev will answer questions, only for a tier 3 dev to come in after a contract is awarded" (UK Business Forums, 29 Jun 2021, https://www.ukbusinessforums.co.uk/threads/how-to-choose-freelance-website-developer-on-upwork.413487/); the "bait and switch" where seniors sell and juniors build (36 Visuals). Observed and practitioner opinion. The same Upwork buyer had "63 proposals" to sift, which is the marketplace's own problem.
10. Check the studio's own site. "if it loads slowly, looks outdated, or makes it hard to find basic information" walk away (36 Visuals). Practitioner opinion. The home page is judged as example number one.
11. Check reviews on Google, Trustpilot and Bark, and Companies House. Ron Studios, WebKatie. Practitioner opinion. (The owner has excluded legal footer content; this is noted, not recommended.)
12. Bark's buyer guide adds: judge "Previous work, client testimonials, and how the designer interacts with you", and look for "evidence of responsive design across devices". https://www.bark.com/en/gb/web-design/web-design-price-guide/ (practitioner opinion; marketplace)

What this means for a studio with no client work to show (business reasoning)

- Examples must be live URLs that open on a phone, not screenshots. The /examples/aurora route already is one. Label it "Example brief, not a client" on the page and on the route.
- Range is judged across examples, so one template shown once reads as "one look repeated". The honest fix today is one layout in two example brands; at three templates, one brief in three layouts; at ten, the gallery. Never a placeholder as if finished.
- The words on the example are read. They must be specific to the example business and free of filler, because that is exactly what buyers say they check.
- The page itself is the first example. Speed, plain words and no broken bits on the home page carry portfolio weight.
- "Speak to past clients" cannot be met yet and must not be faked. The taster is the substitute: the visitor becomes the reference by testing it with their own sentence.
- "Who does the work" is answered by the one-person structure without naming anyone.

## Part 4. Notes for the planner (business reasoning)

- Pay-first is the root of most horror stories here. The page's order (designs, call, quote, then payment) is the answer to fears 1, 3 and 11 at once, and could be drawn as one line of four steps rather than explained.
- Ownership is the fear with the most words behind it and no answer on the page today. It belongs in the real-build section and in a new FAQ item, and it should be stated as facts about the domain, hosting, code and contract, not as a value.
- The buyer's portfolio test (live URL, opened on a phone, words read, range across examples) is exactly what the Examples section must survive. One template shown once fails the range test; one template in two example brands passes it honestly today.
- The AI question the page does not yet answer is the training one, and the UKBDS 2026 figure (73% uncomfortable) says it is a mainstream worry, not a niche one. The answer must be checked against the provider's terms before it is written.
- The DIY comparison can be fair and still favour the studio: builders win on monthly cost and cancelling; the studio wins on time, ownership and a person. Say both.
- Avoid numbers on outcomes. The sourced figures show adoption, not results; the fear is answered by showing the mechanism on the example design.
